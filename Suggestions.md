# Noosphere Animation Rules

## 1. Style identity

**Descriptive style name:** Geometric character-driven 2D explainer animation.

**Channel direction:** Noosphere cinematic explainer style.

These are descriptive production labels, not formally standardized animation categories.

**Production brief:** Flat vector-style illustration with expressive human characters, geometric environments, layered depth, and cinematic motion design.

Combine geometric visual clarity and rich world-building with human characters and situation-based storytelling. Borrow principles from Kurzgesagt and The Infographics Show while developing an original visual identity.

**Core rule: People carry the story; visual metaphors explain the ideas.**

## 2. Visual design rules

| Element | Required direction |
| --- | --- |
| Characters | Recognizable human silhouettes, simplified anatomy, distinctive clothing and hairstyles. Express emotion through posture, eyebrows, eyes, and hands. |
| Environments | Geometric architecture and props with enough specific detail to establish place and period. |
| Colors | Controlled, rich palettes. Use restrained backgrounds and brighter accents to guide attention. |
| Shading | Mostly flat shapes with selective gradients, shadows, and highlights. |
| Depth | Separate foreground, middle ground, and background. Use restrained parallax and atmospheric separation. |
| Movement | Use character acting for human moments and diagrams, transformations, and visual metaphors for explanations. |
| Camera | Use deliberate framing, occasional tracking shots and reveals, and close-ups when emotion or detail matters. |
| Tone | Approachable and visually playful, with restraint for serious history and technical subjects. |

## 3. Do

1. **Create a visual rulebook before production.** Define palette, character proportions, outline policy, shadow style, typography, and motion behavior. Apply them consistently.
2. **Design around the explanation.** Identify what viewers must understand in each shot before decorating it.
3. **Give each shot one dominant focus.** Direct attention through contrast, scale, framing, and movement.
4. **Use expressive poses.** Prioritize clear silhouettes and purposeful gestures over unnecessary facial detail.
5. **Match movement to meaning.** Use anticipation, easing, follow-through, and selective squash-and-stretch. Give people and mechanical objects different movement behavior.
6. **Mix animation techniques.** Use reusable character rigs for routine shots. Add custom poses and selective frame-by-frame animation for important actions and emotional moments.
7. **Keep technical terms precise.** Explain them through staging and clear visual relationships without weakening the underlying meaning.
8. **Prepare assets for animation.** Separate limbs, facial features, props, and environment layers. Draw hidden areas needed during motion and place pivots deliberately.
9. **Let important ideas settle.** Hold useful diagrams, labels, and reactions long enough to understand them.
10. **Use sound deliberately.** Prioritize voice clarity. Let music and effects support changes in action, scale, and mood.

## 4. Don't

1. **Do not copy another channel's signature characters, mascots, or compositions.** Build an original design system from the underlying principles.
2. **Do not confuse constant motion with good animation.** Moving everything competes with the explanation.
3. **Do not make every color equally saturated.** Preserve quiet areas so important elements stand out.
4. **Do not overcomplicate characters.** Excessive detail makes consistent drawing and animation harder.
5. **Do not force every action through one rig.** Repeated generic gestures make characters feel interchangeable.
6. **Do not apply cinematic effects everywhere.** Heavy glow, blur, grain, and camera movement can obscure clean artwork.
7. **Do not animate false relationships.** Arrows can imply causation, size can imply quantity, and timelines can imply certainty. Match those choices to the facts.
8. **Do not show a paragraph while narrating a different one.** Prefer concise labels and deliberate reading time.
9. **Do not force comedy into serious moments.** Adjust expression, pacing, and color to suit the subject.
10. **Do not judge animation from still frames alone.** Review transitions, pacing, legibility, and comprehension at playback speed.

## 5. Scene construction

Use the following sequence when it serves the explanation; do not force every scene into the same pattern.

1. Establish the person, setting, or situation.
2. Introduce the question, conflict, or consequence.
3. Move into a diagram or visual metaphor to explain the mechanism.
4. Return to the human situation to show why the mechanism matters.

Keep character scenes and explanatory graphics consistent in shape language, color, and motion.

### Example: Berkeley and Unix

Show a researcher at a simplified, period-appropriate terminal. Move into the screen and turn the source distribution into a clear diagram. Show copies moving between institutions, with licensing conditions attached to the relevant exchanges. Return to the researcher as the consequences become personal.

The human scene establishes why viewers care. The diagram makes the mechanism understandable. Ensure the depicted relationships match the researched history.

## 6. Production checklist

- [ ] Each shot has a clear explanatory or storytelling purpose.
- [ ] Characters, environments, colors, typography, and shading follow the visual rulebook.
- [ ] The viewer can identify the dominant focus immediately.
- [ ] Poses and gestures communicate the intended action or emotion.
- [ ] Motion and camera movement support comprehension.
- [ ] Diagrams preserve factual and technical accuracy.
- [ ] Labels are readable at the intended viewing size and playback speed.
- [ ] Important ideas have enough time to register.
- [ ] Voice remains clear over music and sound effects.
- [ ] Serious moments receive appropriate visual and emotional treatment.
- [ ] The full sequence has been reviewed in motion, not only as still frames.
- [ ] The result has its own visual identity.

**Overall direction: Geometric illustration + expressive human acting + precise explanatory graphics + restrained cinematic staging.**
